Spline Calculus

How to apply integration and differentiation to one-dimensional polynomial splines.

Area Under the Curve

Let a piecewise polynomial one-dimensional periodic spline of nonnegative integer degree \(n\) and positive integer period \(K\) be \(f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto f(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,c[{k\bmod K}]\,\beta^{n}(x-\delta x-k),\) where \({\mathbf{c}}=\left(c[k]\right)_{k=0}^{K-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\) is its vector of spline coefficients and where \(\delta x\) is its delay. The synthesis function of the spline \(f\) is the B-spline \(\beta^{n}.\) We want to compute the quantity

\[\int_{a}^{b}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\]

with integration bounds \(a\in{\mathbb{R}}\) and \(b\in{\mathbb{R}}.\)

Anti-Gradient

Consider the spline \(f\) above. While it is periodic, its generic integral \(\int_{0}^{x}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\) is not necessarily periodic in terms of \(x.\) Indeed, the integral \(\int_{0}^{K+x}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\) can be segmented in two parts as \(\int_{0}^{K}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\int_{K}^{K+x}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.\) It is then equal to \(K\,E\{f\}+\int_{0}^{x}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\) which matches \(\int_{0}^{x}\,f(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\) only when the mean of \(f\) vanishes.

The anti-gradient of \(f\) is defined as the function

\[S_{f}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto S_{f}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\,\left(f(t)-E\{f\}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.\]

It turns out to be a periodic spline of degree \(n+1.\) As such, it can be handled by the splinekit library. Its most relevant property is that the gradient of the anti-gradient of a spline is the spline itself, up to an additive constant, so that \(\dot{S}_{f}=\left(f-E\{f\}\right).\)

We provide now a few lines of code that first create a random spline of specified period, degree, and delay, and then show its anti-gradient, along with the gradient of this anti-gradient.

Gradient

Consider the \(K\)-periodic spline

\[f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto f(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,c[{k\bmod K}]\,\beta^{n}(x-\delta x-k),\]

with \(n\in{\mathbb{N}}+1\) a positive degree. The spline gradient is its first derivative

\[\dot{f}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto \dot{f}(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,c[{k\bmod K}]\,\dot{\beta}^{n}(x-\delta x-k).\]

Because B-splines of positive degree satisfy the relation \(\dot{\beta}^{n}(x)=\left(\beta^{n-1}(x+\frac{1}{2})-\beta^{n-1}(x-\frac{1}{2})\right)\) for all \(x\in{\mathbb{R}},\) the gradient of a spline is again a spline, albeit one whose degree is smaller and whose delay is offset by \(\frac{1}{2}.\) As such, the gradient can be handled by the splinekit library.

The centered finite difference of a function \(f\) is parameterized by a step \(h\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}\) and is defined as the quantity \(\frac{1}{2\,h}\,\left(f(x+h)-f(x-h)\right).\) At those arguments \(x\in{\mathbb{R}}\) where the function is differentiable, one has that \(\dot{f}(x)=\lim_{h\rightarrow0}\frac{1}{2\,h}\,\left(f(x+h)-f(x-h)\right).\)

We provide now a few lines of code that first create a random spline of specified period, degree, and delay, and then show its true gradient, along with the approximation of the gradient provided by centered finite differences of rational step \(h=\frac{1}{m},\) with \(m\in{\mathbb{N}}+1\) a positive integer.

Derivative of Positive Integer Order

Given the spline \(f\) as above, we want now to compute its derivative of order \(m,\) with \(m\in{\mathbb{N}}+1.\) This derivative will be a true function whenever the degree of the spline is large enough, with \(n\in{\mathbb{N}}+m;\) more precisely, it is going to be a spline of degree \(\left(n-m\right).\)

We propose two approaches to compute the derivative. The first one is useful to evaluate \(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{m}f(x)}{{\mathrm{d}}x^{m}}\) at a single given argument \(x,\) at a low computational cost, but the computational efficiency is lost if this approach is to be repeated for several arguments; the second approach is useful to establish the spline derivative \({\mathrm{D}}^{m}\{f\}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\) as an instance of the PeriodicSpline1D class. This second approach is the one that is implemented in the splinekit library.

Derivative at a Single Argument

In the first method, we choose to proceed with the linear-algebra equivalent representation of the spline as

\[f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto f(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,c[{k\bmod K}]\,\beta^{n}(x-\delta x-k)={\mathbf{c}}_{r}^{{\mathsf{T}}}\,{\mathbf{W}}^{n}\,{\mathbf{v}}^{n}(\chi(x)).\]

There, the partial vector of coefficients \({\mathbf{c}}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\) is defined as \({\mathbf{c}}_{r}=\left(c[{\left(k-r\right)\bmod K}]\right)_{k=0}^{n},\) where \(r=\left\lceil\xi(x)\right\rceil\in{\mathbb{Z}}\) with \(\xi(x)=\left(\frac{n-1}{2}-x-\delta x\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}.\) The spline evaluation matrix \({\mathbf{W}}^{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\left(n+1\right)\times\left(n+1\right)}\) depends on \(n\) only. Finally, the Vandermonde vector \({\mathbf{v}}^{n}(\chi(x))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\) is computed as \({\mathbf{v}}^{n}(\chi(x))=\left(1,\left(\chi^{k}(x)\right)_{k=1}^{n}\right),\) with \(\chi(x)=\left(r-\xi(x)\right)\in[0,1).\)

With these definitions, the derivative of order \(m\) is

\[{\mathrm{D}}^{m}\{f\}(x)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{m}f(x)}{{\mathrm{d}}x^{m}}={\mathbf{c}}_{r}^{{\mathsf{T}}}\,{\mathbf{W}}^{n}\,{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}^{n}\,\left(\left(0\right)_{k=0}^{m-1},{\mathbf{v}}^{n-m}(\chi(x))\right),\]

where \({\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}^{n}={\mathbf{diag}}(\left(\frac{k!}{\left(k-m\right)!}\right)_{k=0}^{n}).\) This allows one to precompute \({\mathbf{W}}^{n}\,{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{m}^{n}\) since it depends neither on the delay of the spline nor on the spline coefficients. Furthermore, the fact that there are leading zeros in the vector \(\left(\left(0\right)_{k=0}^{m-1},{\mathbf{v}}^{n-m}(\chi(x))\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\) gives the opportunity of additional computational savings.

Derivative as a Function

The gradient of a polynomial B-spline of positive integer degree \(n\in{\mathbb{N}}+1\) turns out to be the function \(\dot{\beta}^{n}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto\left(\beta^{n-1}(x+\frac{1}{2})-\beta^{n-1}(x-\frac{1}{2})\right).\) This recursive relation between a B-spline of degree \(n\) and shifted B-splines of degree \(\left(n-1\right)\) can be taken advantage of to express derivatives of higher order, for instance \(\ddot{\beta}^{n}\) or \(\dddot{\beta}^{n}.\) After some algebraic manipulations, it leads one to express the derivative of order \(m\) of a \(K\)-periodic spline as

\[{\mathrm{D}}^{m}\{f\}(x)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{m}f(x)}{{\mathrm{d}}x^{m}}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\Delta_{K}^{m}\{{\mathbf{c}}\}[{k\bmod K}]\,\beta^{n-m}(x-\left(\delta x-\frac{m}{2}\right)-k),\]

where the periodic finite-difference operator \(\Delta_{K}^{m},\) applied to the vector \({\mathbf{c}}=\left(c[k]\right)_{k=0}^{K-1}\) of the spline coefficients, is defined by

\[\forall k\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\Delta_{K}^{m}\{{\mathbf{c}}\}[k]=\sum_{q=0}^{m}\,\left(-1\right)^{q}\,{m\choose q}\,c[{\left(k-q\right)\bmod K}].\]

By identification of terms, it can be seen that the derivative of a periodic spline is indeed a periodic spline of period \(K,\) of degree \(\left(n-m)\right),\) of delay \(\left(\delta x-\frac{m}{2}\right),\) and of spline coefficients \(\left(\Delta_{K}^{m}\{{\mathbf{c}}\}[k]\right)_{k=0}^{K-1}.\)

Derivatives and Fourier Coefficients

Suppose we have at our disposal the spline \(g={\mathrm{D}}^{m}\{f\}\) that results from the \(m\)-th order differentiation of the spline \(f.\) On one hand, we know that the function splinekit.PeriodicSpline1D.fourier_coeff provides us with a direct access to the Fourier coefficients \(F\{g\}.\) On the other hand, the theory asserts that they are related to \(F\{f\}\) as

\[\forall\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}:F\{g\}[\nu]=\left({\mathrm{j}}\,\nu\,\frac{2\,\pi}{K}\right)^{m}\,F\{f\}[\nu].\]
